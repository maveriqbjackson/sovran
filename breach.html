Companies get breached and the contents end up in circulation. Most people have no idea their old password, home address or date of birth is sitting in a dump from 2019. This checks an email address against every publicly known breach.

This one has to leave your device, and here is exactly how far Checking an address against a breach corpus means the address has to reach the corpus. Unlike the Photo Check or the Audit, this cannot run in your browser alone — so instead of pretending otherwise, here is the whole path.

The address goes to our server and straight out to Have I Been Pwned, the breach database maintained by the security researcher Troy Hunt since 2013. We never write it down. Not to our database, not to a log line, not for a moment longer than the request takes. We count how many lookups a client makes so the service cannot be hammered, and that counter holds a number, never an address. Nothing about your result is stored anywhere.

HIBP's policy is that searches are not logged. We link you to their policy rather than paraphrasing it, because you should not have to take our word for someone else's behaviour.
What we add to it

The breach data is theirs and we credit it plainly. What happens next is ours.

  • A priority order. Knowing you appear in fourteen breaches is not useful on its own. Knowing which password to change first, and why that matters more than the rest, is.
  • Severity, marked. A leaked newsletter signup is not a leaked date of birth. We flag the data classes that actually create risk, rather than listing them all as equals.
  • Cover on a shared screen. Sensitive breaches — dating, adult, health — stay hidden until you ask for them. Nobody should have that appear over their shoulder.
  • The connection to everything else. A leaked address is usually a broker listing too. Every result links to the tool that deals with it.

Checking once is free. Being told is the part that costs.

This page is a snapshot. It tells you where you have leaked up to today, and it will stay free permanently — a one-off lookup costs us almost nothing, and charging for it would mean charging you to find out you were harmed.

What costs money is watching. Breaches surface months or years after they happen. Being told within a day that your address appeared in something new — and getting the priority order for it immediately — means someone has to be checking continuously on your behalf. That is ongoing work with an ongoing bill, which is the only reason anything here has a price.
