See what sees you.

Plate readers, traffic cameras, and public surveillance, mapped by volunteers into a public database. Most of it sits on ordinary streets and almost nobody has been told it is there.

Free forever, and staying that way

This map is public records mapped by volunteers. We did not create it and we do not own it, so we are never going to charge you for looking at it. Knowing what is watching your street is not a premium feature.

What SOVRAN+ will add later is the work on top: being told when a new reader appears near somewhere you care about, having public records requests filed for you, and getting retention terms and council agendas tracked without you having to watch for them. That is ongoing labour with a real cost. The map underneath it stays free.

This page is different, and we want you to know why

This page normally makes no outside request at all. We keep a snapshot of the camera data on our own servers, so choosing a region loads it straight from this site and works with your connection switched off, exactly like everything else here.

An outside request happens only in two cases: a region we have not built a snapshot for yet, or you pressing check for newer. Both are your choice, and here is precisely what they send.

What gets sent: a request to overpass-api.de, a free public OpenStreetMap service, asking for every surveillance device inside the bounding box of the region you choose. That is a rectangle roughly the size of a US state.

What does not get sent: your address, your location, your postcode, or anything you type. We never ask for browser location permission. There is no address search, on purpose — typing your street into a box is how these tools usually leak the thing they claim to protect.

And no map tiles. A normal map downloads image tiles as you pan and zoom, which tells the tile server exactly where you are looking and how closely. That is a sharper location trail than asking for your GPS. We draw the map ourselves from coordinates instead. It looks less like Google and more like a radar screen, which is fitting.

SOVRAN receives nothing. The request goes from your browser straight to OpenStreetMap. It does not pass through us and we have no log of it.

Take it further

A dot on a map is awareness. The questions below are what actually changes anything — and they are all public record requests any resident can make.

  • How long is footage kept, and who decides when it is deleted?
  • Which other agencies can query this data, and is any of it shared federally?
  • What is the contract value, and when does it come up for renewal?
  • Was there a public vote, and is there an audit of how searches are used?
How to ask your council →

Found one that isn’t here? Add it.

Every dot on this map was put there by a person who noticed something and wrote it down. Blank areas do not mean quiet streets — they mean nobody has looked yet. This is the rare civic problem where an hour of your time genuinely moves the number.

  1. Note it from the pavement. A plate reader is usually a small camera on a pole, often solar-powered with a black rectangular box, pointed along the roadway rather than at a building. Never enter private property, never climb anything, and never argue with anyone about it. Photographing infrastructure from a public place is lawful in the US; a confrontation helps nobody.
  2. Get the location. The corner it sits on is enough. If you can also see a manufacturer name on the housing, or a city or agency logo, note that too — the operator is the field most often missing, and the most useful one.
  3. Add it to OpenStreetMap. Go to openstreetmap.org, make a free account, find the spot, and press Edit. Drop a point and search the feature list for “surveillance”.
  4. Or use DeFlock. The DeFlock project has a simpler submission flow built for exactly this, and it writes into the same database. If the editor feels fiddly, start there.

The tags that matter, if you are editing directly. Only the first is required — everything else is a bonus.

man_made=surveillanceThe base tag. Without it, nothing finds the device.
surveillance:type=ALPRMarks it as a plate reader. Use camera for ordinary CCTV.
operator=Who runs it — a city, a police department, a company. The most valuable and most often blank.
manufacturer=The brand on the housing, if visible.
direction=Which way it faces, in compass degrees or as N/S/E/W.
surveillance=publicThat it watches a public space rather than a private interior.
Why this is worth an hourSurveillance infrastructure is usually approved in a routine council vote with nobody in the room, and stays invisible because no one has counted it. A map is the thing that turns “I think there might be some cameras” into a number a journalist or a council member has to answer for. It works because it is public, open, and belongs to nobody — which is also why we will never put it behind a payment.
